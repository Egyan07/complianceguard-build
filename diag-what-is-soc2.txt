What is SOC 2?
An accessible guide to the AICPA framework for SaaS founders: report types, the Trust Services Criteria, evidence, and timelines.
Last updated · April 2026
SOC 2 is a security framework developed by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA). It defines criteria for managing customer data across five Trust Services Categories: Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, and Privacy.
Why SOC 2 matters for SaaS companies
Enterprise buyers and their legal teams increasingly require SOC 2 before signing contracts. In procurement, the report has become a pass/fail gate: a security questionnaire that asks for a SOC 2 report and receives nothing usually ends the conversation, regardless of how strong the product is. For companies selling into healthcare, finance, or government-adjacent markets, it is frequently the single fastest way to unblock a stalled deal.
The report also does real work beyond sales. Preparing for one forces the habits that matter anyway — least-privilege access, encrypted devices, monitored changes, tested incident response. Teams that complete a first audit almost universally describe the same outcome: the security posture improved, and it stayed improved because the controls are now evidenced rather than assumed.
Who requires SOC 2?
Nothing in law mandates SOC 2 the way HIPAA mandates safeguards for health data — it is a market expectation, not a regulation. In practice, three groups ask for it: enterprise procurement teams, security reviewers during vendor onboarding, and cyber-insurance underwriters, who increasingly treat it as a signal that basic controls exist. If your customers have customers of their own (a B2B SaaS selling to other B2B SaaS), the request tends to arrive earlier than founders expect.
The flip side is scope: SOC 2 covers the systems
you
operate in delivering your service — your cloud accounts, your laptops, your CI/CD — not your customers' environments and not your product's security properties in the abstract. Agreeing that boundary with your auditor before fieldwork starts is the single highest- leverage decision in the whole process.
SOC 2 Type I vs Type II
There are two types of SOC 2 reports, and the distinction matters to buyers.
Type I
Assesses whether your controls are suitably designed at a single point in time. Faster to obtain; a common first milestone.
Type II
Assesses whether those controls operated effectively over a period of time, typically 3–12 months. Most enterprise prospects require Type II.
Most first-time audits start with a Type I: it is faster, cheaper, and proves your control design is sound. The Type II observation window then runs in the background while you keep operating, and the follow-up report comes with real operating evidence behind it. What buyers read as "having SOC 2" is almost always the Type II report covering the trailing 6–12 months.
The Trust Services Criteria
SOC 2 audits are conducted against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria, the framework that defines what auditors evaluate. The criteria span areas including logical access controls, encryption, audit logging, incident response, vendor management, and change management. Security is the only mandatory category; the other four are added based on the commitments you make to customers.
Security
— the common criteria every report includes: access controls, encryption, logging, incident response, vendor and change management.
Availability
— uptime and capacity commitments you make in an SLA.
Processing Integrity
— that processing is complete, valid, accurate, timely, and authorised.
Confidentiality
— information designated as confidential is protected as promised.
Privacy
— personal information is collected, used, retained, and disposed of according to your notice.
Scoping the categories is a business decision before it is a technical one: every category you add is another set of criteria an auditor will test, so most teams start with Security alone and add categories only when a customer contract explicitly commits to them.

What evidence do auditors need?
Auditors need documented proof that your controls exist and work — and they work from samples, not summaries. Expect to be asked for things like: the password and MFA policy alongside proof it is enforced; firewall rules and disk-encryption status from actual machines; a user access review with dates and approvals; audit logs showing who accessed what; change-management records tying deployments to reviews; AWS configuration snapshots; and an incident-response procedure with a drill or tabletop exercise behind it.
The pattern across every request is the same: an assertion ("we enforce MFA") plus machine-generated proof (the identity provider configuration showing it). Evidence that only exists as a policy document is a finding waiting to happen, because the auditor's next question is always "show me it operating."

How long does SOC 2 take?
A SOC 2 Type I audit typically takes 4–8 weeks from starting evidence collection to receiving your report. A Type II audit adds an observation period — commonly 3–6 months for a first report, sometimes 12 — before the auditor can issue the report, because effectiveness has to be demonstrated over time, not at a moment.
The timeline consequence is simple: the observation window starts whenever your evidence collection starts, not when the audit does. Teams that begin collecting evidence before they “need” the report reach their Type II months earlier than those who wait for a prospect to demand one. Starting early is the single most important thing you can do.

Common mistakes first-time audits make
Most failed or painful SOC 2 engagements trace back to the same handful of errors: scoping too broadly (every extra system multiplies evidence work), treating the report as a point-in-time project instead of an operating habit, writing policies nobody follows because auditors test practice not prose, starting the observation window late so the Type II report arrives long after the deal that needed it, and leaving evidence collection to the week of fieldwork when screenshots cannot prove how a control behaved six months ago.
The antidote to all five is the same: collect evidence continuously from the systems themselves, keep scope tight, and review coverage monthly the way you would any other operational metric.

Frequently asked questions
Is SOC 2 a certification?
No. SOC 2 is an attestation report, not a certification: an independent CPA firm examines your controls against the Trust Services Criteria and issues an opinion for a defined period. Vendor claims of being 'SOC 2 certified' are technically inaccurate — the correct phrasing is 'SOC 2 compliant' or 'has a SOC 2 report'.
What is the difference between SOC 2 Type I and Type II?
A Type I report evaluates whether your controls are suitably designed at a single point in time. A Type II adds operating effectiveness over a period, typically 3 to 12 months. Most enterprise buyers require Type II, because a design-only report proves the control exists but not that it actually works day to day.
Which Trust Services Categories do I need?
Security is mandatory in every SOC 2 report. Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, and Privacy are additive: include each one only where you make explicit commitments to customers about it. Every extra category adds scope, cost, and evidence — scoping tightly is normal and expected for a first audit.
How long does a SOC 2 audit take?
A Type I engagement typically runs 4 to 8 weeks from readiness to report. A Type II requires an observation window — commonly 3 to 12 months — before fieldwork can begin, so teams that start evidence collection early reach their Type II report much sooner than those who wait for the observation period to start.

Do auditors accept automatically collected evidence?
